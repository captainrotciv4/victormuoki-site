Kenya Ethanol Pathway

A proposal to turn Kenya's under-used sugar and maize value chains into clean cooking fuel and locally blended biofuel, adapting what worked in Brazil to Kenyan conditions.

83M LAnnual ethanol processing capacity in Kenya
26.5M LProduced per year: under a third of capacity
2028National target: universal access to clean cooking
2025Energy (Biofuels) Regulations: phased local blending

Status: proposal stage. Concept first developed in April 2025; updated November 2026 with current policy and capacity data.

1. The problem

Most Kenyan households still cook with firewood, charcoal or kerosene. The smoke harms health, especially for women and children, and charcoal demand drives forest loss. At the same time Kenya imports nearly all of its liquid fuel, so every rise in world oil prices lands on transport and food costs.

The sugar sector sits right next to a solution. Ethanol made from sugarcane, molasses or maize burns cleanly in modern stoves and can be blended into petrol. Yet Kenya's ethanol plants produce only about 26.5 million litres a year against roughly 83 million litres of installed capacity, and many sugarcane farmers face low yields and late payment.

3. The proposal: six workstreams

3.1 Dual-feedstock ethanol hubs

Upgrade selected plants in the western sugar belt to process cane juice and molasses in season and maize off-season. Start with molasses, which is a by-product today, before any new crop is diverted.

3.2 Second-crop maize and small-scale irrigation

Encourage sequential maize after cane, supported by small-scale irrigation, so land produces more without competing with food production. Maize for ethanol should come only from surplus or second-crop output.

3.3 Policy fit

Treat denatured cooking ethanol separately from beverage alcohol in tax and licensing, so clean cooking fuel is not priced like spirits. Align with the Energy (Biofuels) Regulations, 2025 and the national Ethanol Cooking Fuel Masterplan.

3.4 Last-mile distribution and finance

Neighbourhood ethanol dispensers, and stove financing that households repay in small amounts over mobile money. This is where the pathway meets my payments and BioCredit work: affordable access depends on small, flexible payments.

3.5 Farmer cooperatives

Aggregate feedstock, share mechanisation and agronomy training, and pay farmers faster through digital payments, so supply is reliable and incomes improve.

3.6 Measurement

Track litres produced, households served, and charcoal or kerosene displaced, so impact can be verified and, later, financed through results-based climate finance.

5. Who needs to be at the table

Ministry of Energy and Petroleum · Energy and Petroleum Regulatory Authority (EPRA) · Kenya Bureau of Standards (KEBS) · Sugar Board and the agriculture ministry · county governments in the sugar belt · sugar companies and distillers · farmer cooperatives · clean-cooking companies and stove makers · climate-finance partners.

6. Phased roadmap

PHASE 1

County feasibility study: feedstock, distillation capacity, demand, and stove distribution in one sugar-belt county.

PHASE 2

Pilot: molasses-based cooking ethanol, a set of neighbourhood dispensers, and mobile-money stove financing.

PHASE 3

Scale: add second-crop maize at hub plants, expand distribution, and bring in results-based finance.
